Many of the installation calls we get in Altoona, PA are not replacements at all. A family adds a bathroom and needs more capacity. A homeowner wants the heater out of a hall closet and into the garage or basement. A remodel needs a second heater on the far side of the house, or a move to all-electric swaps a gas unit for a heat pump model. Every one of them involves new work: gas lines that must be sized for the load, vents that must be routed and pitched correctly, circuits that must match the heater, and water lines that must be run cleanly. Altoona Water Heater works all of that out before the installation starts.

Sizing and placement shape most of the outcome. For a tank, the first-hour rating is the number we compare against your household's busiest hour; for a tankless unit, we size by flow rate and winter temperature rise. Then comes placement. Gas heaters need a proper vent route and combustion air, heat pump heaters need open space and a condensate drain, and whole-house electric tankless units often need a large dedicated circuit and the panel capacity to match. Put a heater above a finished ceiling and it needs a drain pan; put a gas unit in a garage and code may require a raised platform. We explain the trade-offs plainly and without pressure.

The final stretch is where a careful installer and a fast one part ways. Every gas joint is leak-tested, vent pitch and clearances follow the manufacturer's spec, the circuit and breaker match the heater, and dielectric fittings separate copper from steel. The relief valve discharge line runs full size to where it belongs, and expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps are installed when code requires them, with the permit handled when needed. Warning Signs to Watch For

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Remodel plans that add fixtures or a second bathroom
  • Long waits for hot water at faucets far from the heater
  • Morning showers that run cold for the last person
  • A new jetted tub that outpaces the heater's capacity
  • A tank in a tight spot that is hard to service or replace
  • A property where the water heater has to go in from scratch
  • A planned switch from gas to electric, or to a heat pump model
  • An older install without an expansion tank, pan, or straps where required

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

A new water heater installation is really several trades in one job. Gas piping has to be sized for the heater's input and every other appliance on the line, then pressure-tested and leak-free. Venting has to follow the manufacturer's rules for diameter, length, pitch, and clearance to combustibles, or carbon monoxide can spill back into the house. Electric and heat pump units need a circuit, breaker, and wire gauge matched to their load, and whole-house electric tankless units can draw more current than many panels have to spare. On the water side, there is more: shutoffs, dielectric transitions, a correctly rated relief valve with a full-size discharge line, and code items like expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps that differ from place to place.

What you get from a pro is an installation that is done once. Our technicians plan the gas line, vent, and circuit before the first cut, take care of the permit when your jurisdiction requires one, and install the relief valve discharge and code items correctly. Every joint is tested, every gas install is checked for draft and carbon monoxide, and the temperature is set to protect against scalding. Hurried installs leave behind undersized gas lines, poor vent pitch, and missing expansion tanks. Ours are built to pass inspection and to last. And if the simpler option is the right one for your home, we say so plainly.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Tankless heaters need a yearly flush to clear scale from the heat exchanger
  • Keep the temperature around 120 degrees; hotter settings raise the risk of scalding
  • Check the drain pan and relief valve discharge line now and then for signs of water
  • Store paint, gasoline, and solvents far from any gas water heater
  • Start a flushing routine early, before sediment has a chance to harden
  • Leave working space around the heater, and never stack storage against it
  • Schedule regular maintenance, including an anode rod check
